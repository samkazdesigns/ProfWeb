Automatic Pet Feeder

The "Feed & Launch" (F&L) device — a cost-effective robot that automatically refills food and water bowls and entertains a pet by launching a toy when it approaches, driven by two PIC microcontrollers.

01 — OVERVIEW

Overview

For the ME 522 Mechatronics course, the team designed an electromechanical device to help care for a house pet. The result — the F&L (Feed and Launch) device — automatically feeds a pet (food and water) and entertains it by launching a toy. Such a device saves an owner time when they can't be home to feed or play with their pet. I served as project coordinator and CAD modeler.

02 — OBJECTIVE & CONSTRAINTS

Objective & Constraints

The brief required an electromechanical pet-assistance device that fits within an 18 × 18 × 18 inch envelope and includes a defined set of subsystems:

  • A data input and a display
  • An automatic sensor and an actuator
  • Logic and control
  • An audio output
  • A mechanical design

The control logic is state-based: at rest, the weight of food and water presses pushbuttons beneath each bowl, signaling "full." When a bowl is consumed below a threshold, the button releases and the controller actuates a servo to refill it. Separately, a distance sensor watches for the pet — once it approaches and then leaves, a buzzer sounds and the toy launches.

03 — MECHANICAL DESIGN

Mechanical Design

The mechanical design has four parts: the chassis, the food and water compartments, the dispensing system, and the launcher.

  • Chassis & storage. The body houses two 3.8-inch-square compartments. Inverted-triangle openings at their base funnel food and water into the bowls below.
  • Dispensing system. Hinged lids cover each opening; a positional servo at each hinge opens the lid to refill an empty bowl and closes it once full.
  • Launcher. A catapult-style arm carries the toy in a basket, held by an elastic loop hooked to the arm. A continuous servo turns a lever that slowly tightens the loop until it slips and snaps the arm forward, flinging the toy.

04 — ELECTRONICS & CONTROL

Electronics & Control

The system needs 3 inputs and 8 outputs: three servos (two positional for food/water, one continuous for the launcher), four LEDs, a buzzer, two pushbuttons, and one analog distance sensor. Because the Sharp 2Y0A21 sensor returns an analog signal, the team split the work across two PIC16F88 microcontrollers — one dedicated to reading the sensor through its onboard ADC, and a main PIC running everything else.

FIG. 2

FIG. 2 — Wiring diagram. The main PIC drives the three servos, LEDs, and buzzer; the secondary PIC handles the analog distance sensor. Servos are powered separately from the PICs.

Servos are driven by PWM (50 Hz) generated with the PULSOUT command, whose timing scales with the PIC's 8 MHz clock. Each servo was characterized individually because their usable pulse-width ranges differed (e.g. Futaba 0.4–2.4 ms, Parallax continuous 0.5–2.05 ms). The main PIC loops through three checks — refill food, refill water, launch toy — blinking all LEDs each cycle as a debugging heartbeat; the secondary PIC blinks an LED while the pet is within ~5–15 cm and signals the main PIC to launch once it leaves.

05 — RESULTS

Results

In the demonstration, the subsystems worked: the servo-actuated lids opened and closed from the pushbutton pressure plates, the continuous-servo launcher released and fired the test toy, all four LED indicators behaved as designed, and the cardboard chassis stayed sturdy under the servo loads.

Scoped honestly, the team couldn't run the fully integrated machine end-to-end: the servos only behaved once given a power supply separate from the PICs, and driving the whole system from two PIC16F88s — new hardware for the team versus the Arduino they knew — proved challenging. The launcher was also reset by hand. The result was a semi-functional prototype that proved each mechanism individually.

FIG. 5 — The physical prototype on the bench: cardboard chassis with the launcher arm, wired to breadboards carrying the two PICs, servos, and sensor.

06 — LESSONS LEARNED

Lessons Learned

  • Power actuators separately. The servos only ran reliably once isolated from the PICs' supply — a power-distribution lesson that cost the integrated demo.
  • Match the controller to the team's experience. Coding two PIC16F88s in PICBASIC PRO was a steep curve; mixing in a familiar Arduino could have produced a more complete result.
  • Plan for analog signals early. The analog distance sensor forced a dedicated second PIC for ADC — worth designing in from the start.
  • Test parts with guidance. Experimenting to find servo settings may have damaged some; consulting a more experienced source first protects the hardware.
