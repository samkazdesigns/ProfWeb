P-02 · MEDICAL DEVICES · SEP 2023 — MAY 2024

Earthworm-Inspired Soft Body Robot

Senior capstone: a vein-injectable soft robot concept designed to travel within brain vasculature and mechanically remove blood clots — a single-operator, minimally invasive alternative to catheter-based thrombectomy.

TOOLS

SolidWorks · Nitinol SMA · Arduino

01 — OVERVIEW

Overview

Thrombosis — blood clots that obstruct veins — affects over 900,000 people a year, and is especially dangerous as cerebral venous thrombosis (CVT) in the brain, where it can cause headaches, loss of consciousness, and death. Existing treatments (blood thinners, catheter-guided thrombectomy, thrombolysis) are expensive and demand a highly skilled team operating with extreme precision.

The Earthworm-Inspired Soft Body Robot (ESBR) is a senior-capstone concept for a vein-injectable robot that navigates to the clot and removes it mechanically — a low-cost, minimally invasive alternative a single operator can run. Because a device small enough for brain vasculature can't be built with on-campus tooling, the team built a functional, scaled-up prototype to prove the locomotion principle, improving on the previous year's design.

02 — OBJECTIVE & CONSTRAINTS

Objective & Constraints

The goal was peristaltic (worm-like) locomotion through a blood vessel, driven by segments that contract and expand in sequence. The customer-needs and specification study set the governing constraints:

  • Scale. The final device must fit brain blood vessels (~2–5 mm), which rules out conventional motors — so the actuation method had to scale down. Shape-memory alloy (Nitinol) was selected.
  • Biocompatibility. No thermal, electrical, or chemical harm to vessel walls; all current-carrying components insulated or kept external.
  • Flow & grip. Blood must be able to pass the robot, and a soft exterior must grip the vessel wall without damaging it.
  • Feasibility & speed were weighted highest (5/5), alongside size and softness.

To keep the scaled-up prototype representative, the team matched the Reynolds number of its air-flow test environment to the middle cerebral artery, so the turbulence around the robot mimicked real blood flow.

03 — CONCEPT SELECTION

Concept Selection

Each of the four team members generated a concept — a lateral SMA-spring worm, a dual-layer SMA "muscle" sack (longitudinal + circular springs), an inverting omni-tread tube (titanium–graphene bilayer), and a micro-tread vehicle. Scored in a weighted decision matrix against the prior year's robot, the two SMA-spring concepts ranked highest and were merged into the final design:

  • Nitinol SMA springs act as the contracting "muscle" — heated by an electric current, they pull the segment's end caps together.
  • An elastic silicone shell acts as the antagonist — its stored elastic energy re-extends the segment once the springs cool.
  • 3D-printed caps anchor the springs and join segments, with a central pass-through tunnel so blood can flow through the robot.

04 — ENGINEERING & ANALYSIS

Engineering & Analysis

Structural. Spring force was estimated from the coil relationship k = d⁴G / 8D³N and verified with SolidWorks FEA. The springs were sized to out-pull the silicone shell (E ≈ 5.75 MPa) so that contraction wins on heating and the shell wins on cooling, with the two forces balanced through several iterations.

Thermal. Nitinol transforms fully to its strong austenite phase above ~80.9 °C and back to soft martensite below ~38.8 °C. Joule heating (P = I²R) drives the heat-up; a numerical heat-transfer model optimized airflow and insulation thickness, predicting a 6.17 s contract-and-extend cycle.

Electrical. Each wire draws ~1.5 A — too much to drive directly from a microcontroller — so an external supply feeds the springs through relays switched by an Arduino/WeMos controller. A TMP36 temperature sensor and an INA219 current sensor provide feedback, with LEDs indicating heating and cooling state.

Fabrication. Springs were wound on a machined steel screw mold (#10 × 28 TPI), heat-treated at 550 °C for 20 minutes, and water-quenched to "program" the coil shape.

FIG. 3

FIG. 3 — Nitinol wire wound onto the machined steel mold before heat treatment at 550 °C. Quenching sets the spring's "remembered" shape, to which it returns when heated above its transition temperature.

05 — PROTOTYPING & ITERATION

Prototyping & Iteration

Alpha prototype. A single spring contracted reliably — 1.5 A pulled it closed in ~6 s (chosen over 2 A for safety and lower power). Early runs exposed real problems: uneven spring lengths caused non-uniform motion, spring heat melted the PLA cap hooks, and the first thin silicone shell collapsed under its own weight.

Beta prototype. Nearly every component was redesigned. The caps gained screw-clamped spring slots with a peg-and-slot feature to stop unwanted rotation; springs were re-wound with looped ends for fast assembly; and the shell moved from silicone caulk to pourable two-part silicone rubber, cast in a 3D-printed PLA outer mold around a meltable wax inner mold, with thickness increased to 4 mm and an oval profile for stiffness.

06 — RESULTS

Results

A single segment demonstrated repeatable contraction (~2–5 mm) under closed-loop control — temperature and current feedback opened the relay at the target point, with the measured current rising to ~1.5 A as the spring reached its trained length. The validated design predicted a ~6.2 s cycle and projected a ~50% reduction in required procedural staff by letting a single operator run the procedure. The team also produced investor-ready deliverables: a business plan, executive summary, and pitch.

Scoped honestly, full three-segment peristaltic motion was only partially achieved — single-segment contraction was demonstrated — and the most ambitious goals (an active water-cooling loop and a Reynolds-matched blood-vessel test rig) were descoped to focus on proving the core actuation.

07 — LESSONS LEARNED

Lessons Learned

  • Source long-lead materials early. The ideal Nitinol wire was unavailable mid-project, forcing a substitute that deformed over repeated cycles.
  • Decouple the shell from the actuator. Making the elastic shell double as the return spring over-constrained both; a separate covering would have relaxed the design requirements.
  • Design around heat from the start. Trapped heat degraded PLA parts later than expected — thermal limits should drive material choice early.
  • Start fabrication and testing sooner. Each silicone shell took roughly a week to make and was defect-prone, leaving little time to iterate.
